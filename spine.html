Load on the lumbar disc

Spine Under Load

In the 1960s Alf Nachemson pushed a pressure needle into people's lumbar discs and asked them to sit, stand, bend, and lift. The result is one of the most quoted figures in spine care — and it's full of surprises (sitting beats standing; a rounded-back lift is brutal). Click a posture and watch the disc pressure change.

Why sitting beats standing

It feels restful, but relaxed sitting raises disc pressure above standing. Sitting flattens the lumbar lordosis and switches off the passive support of a neutral spine, so the disc and muscles carry more. Add a forward lean at a desk and it climbs further. The cue "sit tall / use a lumbar support" is this curve in one sentence. Nachemson; Nordin & Frankel.

Why the load's lever arm is everything

Hold a box far from your body, or round your back to reach it, and its weight acts on a long lever arm from the disc — the back muscles must pull hard to balance it, and nearly all of that force presses straight down through the disc. Keep the load close and hinge at the hips (neutral spine) and the same box costs the disc far less. This is the "lift with your legs" mechanism.

Nuance the students should hear

Nachemson's classic percentages are a teaching model from a small in-vivo sample; Wilke's 1999 restudy refined some values (sitting and standing came out closer). And "high disc pressure" is not the same as "danger" — spines are built to load. Use the ranking to explain mechanics, not to make anyone afraid to bend. Wilke et al. 1999.

Relative pressures are the classic Nachemson values (standing ≈ 100%), as reproduced in Nordin & Frankel and Neumann; the MPa figures assume ~0.5 MPa standing (Wilke) and are approximate. Illustrative teaching model — not a measurement or clinical advice.

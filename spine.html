Load on the lumbar disc

Spine Under Load

In the 1960s Alf Nachemson pushed a pressure needle into lumbar discs and asked people to sit, stand, bend and lift. His figure became one of the most quoted in all of spine care. Thirty years later Wilke and colleagues repeated the measurement with modern telemetry and disagreed with one of its most famous claims. The sitting claim that didn't survive

Every textbook repeats it: relaxed sitting loads the disc more than standing (Nachemson: 140% vs 100%). But when Wilke measured it directly, sitting unsupported came out at 0.46 MPa against 0.50 MPa for standing, slightly lower, and slouched sitting lower still at 0.30. His paper concludes, in his own words, that disc pressure while sitting "may in fact be less than that in erect standing". Toggle the two data sets above and watch the sitting bar cross the standing bar. Sitting still has real costs. It is static, it loads one tissue continuously, and comfort drops. But the claim that sitting crushes your discs is not what the best measurement shows.

What both studies do agree on: the lever arm

Bending forward and holding a load away from the body raises disc pressure hugely, in every data set, by a wide margin. The mechanism is the same one as on the Levers page. The load sits on a long lever arm from the disc. The back extensors pull on a lever arm of only a few centimetres, so they must generate very large forces, and almost all of that muscle force ends up compressing the disc. In Wilke's data, lifting 20 kg with a rounded back reached 2.3 MPa, about 4.6× standing. Lifting the same 20 kg with bent knees and a straight back reached 1.7 MPa. Keeping the load close and hinging at the hips is worth roughly a quarter of the pressure.

How much should you trust either number?

Read the sample sizes. Nachemson's subjects were 8 patients admitted for low back pain or sciatica instead of healthy volunteers, and standing was recorded in only 2 of them. His "bent forward" was a 20° lean, because the needle could not bend further. Wilke's study is a beautiful 24-hour recording of exactly one person. Neither can carry the weight the textbook figure is usually asked to carry. Two things do hold up: the ranking (lying < upright < flexed < flexed with a load) and the order of magnitude. Remember too that high pressure ≠ damage. Discs are load-bearing tissue and they adapt to load. Use these curves to explain mechanics, never to frighten someone out of bending.

Nachemson values are relative load with standing = 100% (L3), from Nachemson 1965 and the summary figure reproduced in Nordin & Frankel and Neumann. Wilke values are absolute nucleus pressure in MPa at L4/5 from Wilke et al. 1999, shown here also as a percentage of that study's own standing value (0.50 MPa).

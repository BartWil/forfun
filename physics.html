    The bit everyone pretends they remember

    Physics, from scratch

    Biomechanics is applied Newtonian mechanics, and almost every other station on this site quietly assumes you are comfortable with it. This page stops assuming. It starts from what a force actually is and builds up, slowly, to the three laws and the one equation you will meet again everywhere. No calculus. No proofs. No steps skipped because they are "obvious".

    Written for the person who scraped a pass in school physics, decided they were "not a maths person", and has been quietly working around that ever since. You were not bad at this. It was taught to you in the wrong order.

    Before anything else

    How to read a formula without panicking

    A formula is not a spell and it is not a test. It is a sentence written in shorthand, and like any sentence it can be read out loud in ordinary words. The only reason formulas feel hostile is that nobody reads them out loud to you. So here is the deal for this page: every single symbol you meet is hoverable. Put your mouse on it, or tap it on a phone, and you get its name, what it means in plain language, its unit, an everyday analogy, a worked example with real numbers, and the mistake people usually make with it.

    Try it now. Here is the equation this whole page is building towards. You are not expected to understand it yet. Just hover the letters and see what happens.

    F = m · a

    Out loud: "the net force on something equals its mass times its acceleration". Five words of shorthand, one sentence of meaning.

    One habit worth building. Whenever you see a formula, do not try to memorise it. Instead ask three questions: what does each letter stand for, what units is it in, and what happens if I make one of them bigger? Those three questions turn a formula from something to be feared into something you can interrogate. Every section below ends with exactly that.
    Concept one

    What a force actually is

    A force is a push or a pull. That is genuinely the whole definition, and it is worth pausing on how ordinary it is. When you press a hand into a patient's shoulder, that is a force. When the floor holds you up, that is a force. When the Earth pulls your body down, that is a force. When a muscle shortens and drags a bone towards another bone, that is a force. Nothing exotic is going on.

    What makes a force slightly more than "a push" is that it has two separate pieces of information bundled into it, and you need both or the description is useless.

    How hard

    The size of the push, measured in newtons. A gentle nudge might be 5 N. Your bodyweight pressing into the floor is several hundred. A sprinter's foot at push-off can exceed 2000 N.

    Which way

    The direction the push points. 200 N straight down and 200 N sideways do completely different things to a body. A quantity carrying both size and direction is called a vector.

    This is why forces in biomechanics are drawn as arrows. The length of the arrow is how hard, the way it points is which way. If you have ever looked at a gait analysis figure and wondered why there is a stick growing out of somebody's foot, that is why: it is the force from the ground, drawn to scale.

    Ask the three questions
    • What is it? A push or a pull, with a size and a direction.
    • What unit? The newton, written N.
    • Make it bigger? The thing you are pushing changes its motion faster. Exactly how much faster is the second law, further down.
    Concept two

    Mass and weight are not the same word

    In everyday speech we use these interchangeably and it causes no harm. In mechanics they are two different quantities with two different units, and mixing them up is the single most common reason a calculation comes out wrong by a factor of about ten. It is worth ten minutes of your life to separate them properly.

    Mass kilograms, kg

    How much stuff there is. How many atoms, roughly. This is a property of the object itself and it does not care where the object is. A 70 kg person is 70 kg on Earth, 70 kg on the Moon, and 70 kg drifting in deep space. Mass is also a measure of how stubborn something is, which turns out to matter enormously and comes back in the second law.

    Weight newtons, N

    The force with which gravity pulls that stuff. It is a force, so it is measured in newtons, and it absolutely does care where you are. The same 70 kg person is pulled with about 687 N on Earth and about 113 N on the Moon, because the Moon pulls more weakly.

    Apparent weight newtons, N

    What a scale actually reads: the force the support pushes back with. Standing still it equals the gravitational force, which is why the two get confused. Accelerate vertically and they part company, and in free fall this one is zero while gravity carries on unchanged.

    The bridge between them is one small formula. You met F = ma at the top of the page, but that was a preview to hover at, not something we used. This is the first one we are actually going to put numbers into. Hover every letter.

    W = m · g

    Out loud: "weight equals mass times the strength of gravity". On Earth g is about 9.81, so to get someone's weight in newtons you take their mass in kilograms and multiply by roughly ten.

    Where this bites in practice. A bathroom scale is labelled in kilograms, so it looks like it measures mass. It does not. It measures the force you press into it and then quietly divides by 9.81 to show you a number you expect. That is harmless standing still. It stops being harmless the moment you are accelerating, which is exactly what a force plate exploits: stand on one and rise up quickly and it will briefly read far more than your "weight", not because you gained mass, but because you added an acceleration on top of gravity.
    And the one everybody gets told wrong at school. Astronauts do not float because there is no gravity up there. A space station orbits a few hundred kilometres up, which is nothing next to the six and a half thousand kilometre radius of the Earth, so gravity there is still about 90% as strong as it is in your kitchen. What has gone to zero is their apparent weight, because they and the station are falling together and nothing is pushing back on them. An orbit is not an escape from gravity. It is falling sideways fast enough to keep missing the planet.
    Ask the three questions
    • What is it? Mass is how much stuff. Weight is the force of gravity on that stuff.
    • What units? Mass in kilograms. Weight in newtons. Never the same unit, never interchangeable.
    • Make mass bigger? Weight goes up in proportion, and the object also becomes harder to get moving. Two separate consequences of the same number.
    Concept three

    Acceleration, which is where most people quietly get lost

    If one idea on this page deserves patience, it is this one. Almost everybody who says they "don't get physics" actually means they never fully separated these three words, because in ordinary English they blur together. Let us pull them apart with an entirely undramatic example: walking down a corridor.

    Position m

    Where you are. Ten metres along the corridor.

    Velocity m/s

    How fast your position is changing. One and a half metres every second.

    Acceleration m/s²

    How fast your velocity is changing. This is the one that feels slippery.

    Here is the sentence to hold on to. Acceleration is not how fast you are going. Acceleration is how quickly your velocity is changing: its size, its direction, or both. That last part matters and is usually left out. A car going round a roundabout at a rock-steady 30 km/h is accelerating the entire way round, because its velocity is turning even though its speed never moves. Velocity and speed are not synonyms, and this is where the difference earns its keep.

    Sitting in a chair

    Velocity zero. Acceleration zero. Nothing is happening, which is the easy case.

    Cruising in a straight line

    Huge velocity. Zero acceleration. You are covering ground fast, but neither your speed nor your direction is changing, so there is no acceleration at all. This is the one people find hardest to accept. Note that forces are still very much present, engine and drag and gravity and road; they simply add up to nothing.

    Lights go green

    Zero velocity. Large acceleration. At the instant you start moving you are not yet going anywhere, but your speed is climbing quickly. This is the mirror image of the case above.

    Braking hard

    Velocity forwards. Acceleration backwards. Slowing down is acceleration too. It simply points the opposite way to your motion. Physics has no separate word for it; "deceleration" is everyday speech, not mechanics.

    Steady speed round a bend

    Constant speed. Real acceleration. The speedometer never moves, but the direction of travel does, so the velocity is changing and that is acceleration. Something must be supplying the force: on a road it is friction at the tyres, pointing towards the centre of the curve. This is the case that a definition mentioning only speed will quietly hide from you.

    These matter more than they look, because in walking and running the interesting forces are mostly the slowing-down ones. Every time your foot lands you are braking, and braking is acceleration pointing backwards, which by the second law means a real force pointing backwards. It is not a metaphor.

    One warning before the demo below. It shows a puck sliding on frictionless ice rather than a person walking, and that is deliberate. Walking at a steady average speed is not zero acceleration. Your centre of mass rises and falls and speeds up and slows within every single step, so there are substantial accelerations throughout, and only the average over a whole stride comes out near zero. Using a walker to illustrate constant velocity would teach you something false about the exact activity this website is mostly about.

    And what on earth is m/s²? It reads "metres per second per second", and the doubled "per second" is not a typo. Velocity is metres per second. Acceleration is how much that changes each second, so it is (metres per second) per second. If your acceleration is 2 m/s², then every second that passes, your speed goes up by 2 m/s. After one second you are doing 2 m/s, after two seconds 4 m/s, after three 6 m/s. That is all the little ² is telling you.
    The centrepiece

    Newton's second law

    Everything above was groundwork for this. The second law is the sentence that connects force, mass and acceleration into one relationship, and once you have it, an enormous amount of biomechanics stops being mysterious. It is also, in fairness, one of the most useful sentences ever written down.

    Fnet = m · a

    Out loud: "the total force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration".

    Read slowly, it says something almost obvious and genuinely powerful: push something harder and it changes speed faster; make something heavier and the same push changes its speed less. You already knew both halves. A shopping trolley full of bricks is harder to get moving than an empty one, and you get it moving quicker if you shove harder. The second law is that intuition, written precisely enough to put numbers in.

    The word doing the most work here is "net"

    This is the detail that trips people up, so it gets its own heading. The F in the formula is not "a force". It is everything pushing on the object, added up as arrows, all at once. Forces that point opposite ways cancel. Only what is left over causes acceleration.

    Right now, sitting still, gravity is pulling you down with several hundred newtons. You are not accelerating downwards. That is not because gravity switched off. It is because the chair is pushing up with an almost exactly equal force, the two cancel, the net force is nearly zero, and so your acceleration is nearly zero. Enormous forces, no motion. The formula handles this perfectly, but only if you remember to add everything up first.

    The classic mistake. Seeing a big force and concluding there must be a big acceleration. There is only a big acceleration if that force is not being cancelled by something else. A patient standing on a force plate registers around 700 N and is going absolutely nowhere.

    The same law, rearranged three ways

    One relationship can be written three ways depending on what you already know and what you are trying to find. This is ordinary algebra and there is nothing hiding in it, but seeing all three next to each other is how it stops feeling like three separate facts to memorise.

    F=m·a

    You know mass and acceleration, you want force. "This 70 kg runner slows by 4 m/s² on landing, so what force went through them?"

    a=Fm

    You know force and mass, you want acceleration. "I push this wheelchair with 90 N, how quickly does it pick up speed?"

    m=Fa

    You know force and acceleration, you want mass. Less common in the clinic, but it is how you weigh things where a scale will not go, including astronauts.

    Now go and play with it

    Reading about a relationship is the slow way to learn it. Below, you can set any two of the three quantities and watch the third follow, with a body that actually moves so you can see what the number means. Push a heavy thing gently. Push a light thing hard. Try to get the same acceleration two different ways.

    The step everyone skips

    Before you use the formula, draw the body

    You can know F = ma perfectly and still get every problem wrong, because knowing the formula does not tell you which forces to put into it. That is a separate skill, it is the one that actually gets used, and it is almost never taught explicitly. It has a name: drawing a free-body diagram.

    The idea is simple. You pick one object, mentally cut it out of the world, and draw only the forces that the outside world applies to that object. Nothing else goes on the drawing. Not the forces it applies to other things, not what is happening inside it, not what it is about to do. Four questions, always in this order:

    1
    What exactly is my body or system?

    The whole person? Just the forearm? The person and the crutches together? There is no right answer until you choose, and every later step depends on the choice. Write it down. Most confusion in biomechanics comes from silently changing this halfway through.

    2
    What external forces act on it?

    Only things touching it, plus gravity, which reaches in without touching. For a standing person that is usually just two: gravity pulling down, and the ground pushing up. If you find yourself drawing more than about four, check whether some of them are really the same force counted twice.

    3
    Which forces belong to some other body?

    This is where the third law does its damage if you are careless. The force your foot puts on the ground is not on your diagram, because it does not act on you. Leave it off. Muscle forces are also usually off, because if your chosen body is the whole person, the muscles are inside it and internal forces cancel in pairs.

    4
    Which way am I calling positive?

    Pick a direction, state it, and stick to it for the whole problem. Up positive or down positive, either is fine, but a sign that changes meaning halfway through is the single most common source of an answer that is right in size and wrong in direction.

    Worked in one line. A 70 kg person stands still. Body: the whole person. External forces: gravity, 687 N down; ground reaction, unknown, up. Not on the diagram: the 687 N their feet press into the floor, which acts on the floor. Positive is up. They are not accelerating, so the net force is zero, so the ground must be pushing up with exactly 687 N. That is the entire calculation, and notice that the hard part was deciding what to draw, not the arithmetic.

    Every inverse dynamics calculation in biomechanics, including the one on the Inverse Dynamics station, is this procedure applied segment by segment up a limb. Get comfortable with the four questions here and that page will feel like more of the same rather than something new.

    Going back for the first

    Newton's first law, and why it comes second here

    Textbooks put this one first. This page does not, because it makes far more sense once you have the second law in your hands. The first law says:

    An object carries on doing exactly what it was already doing, at the same speed in the same direction, unless a net force acts on it.

    Look at what that is, given what you now know. Put zero into the second law: if the net force is zero then a must be zero, and zero acceleration means the velocity never changes. The first law is the second law with the net force set to zero, which is not the same as saying nothing is pushing. Plenty may be pushing, as long as it all cancels.

    There is also a second job the first law quietly does, worth knowing about even if you never use it. It tells you where you are allowed to stand while doing the physics. The laws in this simple form only hold in a frame that is not itself accelerating, called an inertial frame. Do your mechanics from inside a braking bus and objects will appear to accelerate with nothing pushing them, and your sums will come out wrong. This is why a gait laboratory measures against the room and not against something moving.

    What made it revolutionary is that it contradicts everyday experience. Slide a book across a table and it stops, so surely things naturally slow down? They do not. The book stops because friction is pushing against it. Remove the friction, as on ice or in space, and it simply keeps going. Aristotle got this wrong for two thousand years, so if it feels counterintuitive you are in respectable company.

    The word for this stubbornness is inertia, and mass is the measure of it. A heavier body is harder to start moving and harder to stop once moving. This is why a falling patient is dangerous to catch, and why the catching is safer done over a longer time. The reasoning runs through acceleration: the same change in velocity spread over a longer time is a smaller acceleration, and a smaller acceleration needs a smaller force. Physicists package this as impulse, force multiplied by the time it acts, and it is the honest name for what a crumple zone, a crash mat and a well-taught landing all do. They do not reduce how much velocity has to disappear. They give it longer to disappear in.
    The third

    Newton's third law, and the reason walking works

    If A pushes on B, then B pushes back on A, equally hard, in exactly the opposite direction.

    Everyone can recite this one as "action and reaction", and almost everyone misunderstands it in the same specific way, so let us go straight at the misunderstanding.

    "If the forces are equal and opposite, why doesn't everything cancel and nothing ever move?"
    Because the two forces act on two different objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the same object. Your foot pushes the ground, so that force is on the ground. The ground pushes your foot, so that force is on you. They can never cancel each other, because they are not even in the same conversation.

    This is not a technicality. It is the entire reason locomotion is possible. You do not move yourself forwards. You push the planet backwards, and the planet pushes you forwards. In ordinary walking and running that push from the ground is your main source of horizontal external force, and it is precisely what a force plate measures. It is not the only force that can ever move a body, of course: gravity does it to a falling person, water does it to a swimmer, and another person does it when they pull you up from a chair. But on dry land, on your own feet, the ground is what you have. Take the friction away, on ice or a polished floor in socks, and your foot slides instead of pushing, so the ground has nothing to push back with, and you go nowhere.

    The planet, incidentally, does accelerate backwards when you walk. Put the Earth's mass into the second law and the number comes out so close to zero that no instrument will ever see it. The law still holds. The consequence is just very unevenly shared.

    One more, because bodies are levers

    Turning things: the moment of a force

    Everything so far has been about making things move in straight lines. Bodies do not work that way. Bones rotate around joints, so the quantity that matters is not just how hard you push, but how hard you push and how far from the pivot you push it. That combination has a name: the moment of a force, also called torque.

    M = F · d⊥

    Out loud: "the turning effect equals the force times its perpendicular distance from the pivot". The unit is the newton metre, N·m, which is literally the two units multiplied together.

    The word "perpendicular" is not decoration, so it is in the formula rather than hidden in a tooltip. The distance you need is not how far away you grabbed the thing. It is the shortest distance from the pivot to the line the force acts along. Push a spanner sideways and it turns the bolt. Push it along its own length, however hard, and nothing happens at all, because that perpendicular distance has become zero. Same force, same grip point, no turning effect. In the demo below the forearm is kept horizontal and the load hangs straight down, which is the one convenient case where the distance along the forearm and the perpendicular distance are the same number. Tilt the arm and they separate.

    You have used this your whole life without naming it. A door handle is placed as far from the hinge as possible, because the same push gives a much bigger turning effect out there. Try pushing a door open right beside its hinge and it barely moves. The force did not change. The distance did.

    In a body, that distance is usually short and unhelpful. A muscle attaches close to the joint it moves, so its moment arm is small, and it therefore needs a large force to produce a modest turning effect. Be careful with that sentence though: the moment arm is not simply the distance to where the tendon attaches. It depends on the direction the muscle pulls, and it changes continuously as the joint angle changes, which is a large part of why you are stronger at some points in a movement than others. Meanwhile the load in your hand sits far out at the end of a long forearm, with a large distance and a correspondingly large turning effect from a small weight. That geometric unfairness is why muscles pull with forces far greater than the weights they lift, and it is the subject of the Muscle Machine station.

    Rather than assert that, here it is to play with, on a spanner and a bolt before any anatomy gets involved. Set the angle to 90 degrees and the two distances agree, which is the picture every textbook draws. Then drag the angle away from 90 without moving your hand and without changing the force, and watch the moment fall while r stays exactly where it was. At 0 degrees you are pulling along the lever, the line of action runs straight through the pivot, and a 300 N pull produces nothing whatsoever.

    The sentence worth taking away. The moment does not depend on how far from the pivot you grabbed the thing. It depends on the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line the force acts along. Those two are the same number only when you pull at a right angle, and that is exactly the case textbooks draw, which is why so many people finish a physics course never having noticed the difference.

    Now the same thing in an arm

    With that established, the forearm becomes an ordinary application of it rather than a new idea. Below, the arm is held horizontal and the load hangs straight down, which is the convenient 90 degree case where the distance along the forearm and the perpendicular distance are the same number. That is why the arithmetic is easy here, and it is also why a real elbow is harder: rotate the joint and the two separate again, which is a large part of why your strength changes through the range.

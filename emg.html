    What the electrode recorded, and what you did to it

    EMG Lab

    Below is eight seconds of a real person walking, recorded from three muscles at a thousand samples a second. It has not been filtered, rectified, smoothed or scaled. It is the raw electrical mess the electrodes actually saw. Everything that turns that mess into the clean activation curve you see in papers is a choice someone made, and on this page the choices are yours. Make them, then look at what your number is worth.     Work through the seven steps in order. Each one shows you the signal as it stands, and the controls that apply to it. Steps two and six ask you to commit to an answer before you see the result, which is the only way to find out whether you actually believed it.

    Why a filter is never free

    The published analysis of these data used a 50 Hz high-pass, of fourth order, applied forwards and backwards so it adds no time shift, followed by rectification and a 20 Hz low-pass envelope of the same order. This page runs that same filter: the browser's output is checked against a reference implementation and against the closed-form Butterworth response every time the tests run. Those numbers are the authors' choice for their question, not a universal standard for electromyography. A different group analysing the same recording could reasonably pick differently: around 20 Hz is a widely used general-purpose compromise, and the trade is always the same one. A higher cutoff removes more movement artefact, and removes more real muscle signal with it.

    That is why step two draws a third trace: what the filter threw away. It is simply the raw signal minus the filtered one. Watch it while you move the cutoff. At 10 Hz it is nearly flat. At 50 Hz it carries visible bursts that line up with the steps, because a filter cannot tell a muscle from an artefact. It only knows frequency.

    Why the edges are shaded

    Zero-phase filtering runs the filter forwards and then backwards, so every estimate needs signal on both sides of it. At the very start and the very end there is no such signal, so the filter is handed an invented extension of the recording and is partly talking to itself. The first and last half second are shaded for that reason, and every metric on this page is computed on the interior only. The published workflow reaches the same conclusion by a different route: it discards the first and last gait cycle before analysis. Edge effects are not a flaw peculiar to this page. They are a property of the method, and knowing where your numbers stop being trustworthy is part of using it.

    There is no true activation curve

    Step four smooths the rectified signal into an envelope. Try 3, 6, 10 and 20 Hz in turn. At 3 Hz you get a calm, confident-looking hump per step. At 20 Hz you get a jagged thing that keeps changing its mind. Both come from exactly the same muscle. Neither is the real activation, because activation is not a quantity the electrode measured. The envelope is an estimate whose shape you selected, and the papers that show you a beautiful smooth curve have selected one too. They usually say which, in the methods section, in one line most readers skip.

    The denominator decides the number

    Step five is where amplitude gets its units, and where most misreadings are born. Raw millivolts cannot be compared between people, between sessions, or even between two electrodes on the same person, because the voltage that reaches the skin depends on fat, skin impedance and exactly where you stuck the sensor. Peak of trial fixes that by dividing each muscle by its own maximum, which makes timing comparable and makes amplitude meaningless across muscles. Percent MVC divides by a maximal voluntary contraction instead.

    There is a problem with the third one here, and rather than hide it we made it the exercise. No maximal contraction was recorded in this dataset, so the MVC on this page is a slider, not a measurement. It is expressed in the same units as the envelope it divides. Drag it from 0.60 down to 0.10 mV and watch this identical recording report 27%, then 40%, then 80%, then 159% of maximum. Nothing about the muscle changed. Only your denominator did. And when it passes 100, resist the urge to call it impossible: a reference contraction that was not truly maximal does exactly this.

    What this station is actually teaching

    Not electromyography. The habit underneath it: keeping three things apart that are easy to blur together. What the instrument recorded. What the analyst did to it. What may honestly be concluded from the result. On this page you have done all three yourself, which means that the next time you read a figure captioned "muscle activation", you will know how many decisions are sitting behind that curve, and you will know to look for them in the methods.

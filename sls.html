Frontal-plane control

The Single-Leg Squat

Stand on one leg and slowly squat. It sounds trivial, but it's one of the most revealing screens in sports medicine: it exposes whether your hip, knee, and trunk stay stacked and controlled — or whether the knee caves inward into dynamic valgus. Drag the control slider and watch the collapse appear.

What "dynamic knee valgus" is

Watch the knee against the dashed line that runs from hip to foot. Under good control it tracks over the foot. Under poor control it drifts medially — inward, toward the other leg — as the hip rotates and adducts. That knee-in position, combined with a dropped pelvis and a leaning trunk, is the pattern linked in the literature with higher knee-injury risk (ACL, patellofemoral pain).

The three things that give it away

Clinicians and biomechanists don't just eyeball it — they quantify it: the knee's medial displacement / frontal-plane projection angle, the contralateral pelvic drop (the other hip sinking), and the trunk lean. All three tend to grow together as control fails, and all three are visible from the front. Slide the control and watch the three numbers climb together.

How OpenCap measures it

Traditionally this needs a marker-based lab. OpenCap does it from two ordinary phone videos: it estimates body keypoints, triangulates them to 3D, and fits a full musculoskeletal model (Uhlrich, Falisse et al., 2023) — giving the same hip/knee/trunk angles a lab would, from a phone. That's what makes screening like this scalable outside the lab.

The angles, pelvic drop, and trunk lean here are synthetic and idealised for teaching, not measurements of any person. The metric names (medial knee displacement, frontal-plane projection angle, pelvic drop, trunk lean) mirror what a real OpenCap single-leg-squat pipeline computes, but any thresholds shown are illustrative and not a diagnostic cut-off.

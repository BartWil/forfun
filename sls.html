Frontal-plane control

The Single-Leg Squat

Stand on one leg and slowly squat. It sounds trivial, but it makes frontal-plane control unusually easy to see: whether your hip, knee and trunk stay stacked, or whether the knee caves inward into dynamic valgus. Drag the control slider and watch the collapse appear.

Real markerless capture

These are real 3D joint positions from an OpenCap capture, two phone videos turned into a moving skeleton, shown as an anonymized pilot sample (single-leg squat, left stance). It is here to show what the raw motion looks like, so no metrics or interpretation are shown. Notice how cleanly the planted stance leg tracks while the lifted leg stays noisy. That is a real limitation of markerless capture on a limb the cameras cannot fully see.

What "dynamic knee valgus" is

Watch the knee against the dashed line that runs from hip to foot. Under good control it tracks over the foot. Under poor control it drifts medially, inward toward the other leg, as the hip rotates and adducts. That knee-in position, combined with a dropped pelvis and a leaning trunk, has been associated with knee-injury outcomes such as anterior cruciate ligament rupture and patellofemoral pain in some studies and some populations. Read that as a movement characteristic worth describing, not as a screening test: association in a group does not let you predict injury in the person in front of you, and the findings are not consistent across sexes, sports and age groups.

The three things that give it away

Clinicians and biomechanists put numbers on it: the knee's medial displacement and frontal-plane projection angle, the contralateral pelvic drop (the other hip sinking), and the trunk lean. All three tend to grow together as control fails, and all three are visible from the front. Slide the control and watch the three numbers climb together.

How OpenCap measures it

Traditionally this needs a marker-based lab. OpenCap does it from two ordinary phone videos. It estimates body keypoints, triangulates them to 3D, and fits a full musculoskeletal model (Uhlrich, Falisse et al., 2023). That puts three-dimensional analysis within reach of a clinic or a school hall, which is genuinely new. It does not make it equivalent to a marker-based laboratory. Agreement is best for sagittal-plane angles, the flexion and extension you see from the side. Accuracy is poorer and less consistent out of plane, in the frontal and transverse rotations. That caveat lands squarely on this page, because frontal-plane control is exactly what a single-leg squat is used to look at. Trust the shape of the movement more than the last degree of any frontal-plane number.

The angles, pelvic drop and trunk lean here are synthetic and idealised for teaching. They are not measurements of any person. The metric names (medial knee displacement, frontal-plane projection angle, pelvic drop, trunk lean) mirror what a real OpenCap single-leg-squat pipeline computes, but any thresholds shown are illustrative and carry no diagnostic weight. Markerless accuracy is plane-dependent, so frontal-plane values from any markerless system deserve more caution than sagittal ones. Not medical advice.

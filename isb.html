    One shared language

    The ISB Standard

    Before these two papers, every laboratory could measure the same knee, the same shoulder, the same walk, then publish different numbers, because each had chosen its own axes and its own order of rotations. The ISB recommendations fixed the definitions. Almost every motion-capture result you will ever read sits on top of them. Hover any underlined phrase to see exactly what the papers say.

    The problem

    Same movement, different numbers

    A joint angle is not a thing you measure directly. It is a number you calculate, and the calculation needs three decisions: which bony points define each segment, which way the axes point, and in which order the rotations are applied. Change any one and the number changes, while the patient does exactly the same thing. In 1993 the International Society of Biomechanics set up a committee because there was no standard at all, and the consequence was blunt: studies could barely be compared.

    Their answer was to adopt the Joint Coordinate System of Grood and Suntay, built for the knee in 1983, and extend it to the whole body, partly because it reports motion in terms clinicians already use.

    See it for yourself

    Rotations do not commute

    This is the heart of it. Below, one identical segment gets the same two rotations, one about Z and one about X, applied in the two possible orders. Drag the slider and watch the two ends of the movement drift apart. The papers put it plainly: flexion then abduction is not abduction then flexion. When Karduna and colleagues tested every possible sequence for scapular motion, they reported differences of up to 50° for some angles, from nothing but the order.

    This is also why the standard is careful to say that the JCS itself does not depend on sequence, while Euler and Cardan angles do. Choosing a convention is what stands between two labs agreeing and two labs silently disagreeing.

    Before the standard

    Three conventions, one vertebra

    The spine is where this was worst, and Part I says so directly: other axis conventions have been described. Here are the three the paper lists, on the same bone. Only the labels change, never the vertebra. A number reported as "rotation about Z" means three different movements depending on whose paper you are holding.

    The recipe

    How a joint coordinate system is built

    1. 1

      Give every bone its own axes

      Each segment gets a Cartesian system defined from bony points you can palpate or find on an X-ray. That constraint is deliberate, because a definition you cannot locate on a living person is useless in a gait lab. Where a point is not palpable, such as the hip centre, the paper says how to estimate it and keeps to landmarks reachable in vivo.

    2. 2

      Build the joint system from two bones

      The JCS uses one axis fixed in the proximal bone (e1), one fixed in the distal bone (e3), and a third that belongs to neither: two axes are body fixed, one “floats”. The floating axis e2 is simply the common perpendicular of the other two, so it moves as the joint moves.

    3. 3

      Read the three rotations off those axes

      Rotation about e1 is flexion/extension, about the floating e2 is abduction/adduction or lateral bending, about e3 is axial rotation. Three translations come with them. The point of the whole exercise is that everyone then means the same thing.

    In practice

    Three things worth remembering

    Left limbs need mirroring

    The shoulder standard is written for the right side only. Left shoulders should be mirrored about the sagittal plane before the definitions are applied. Forget this and every rotation sign flips.

    The shoulder is the odd one out

    The glenohumeral joint is the only one built on a true Euler sequence (Y–X–Y), because e1 and e3 start out parallel. The usual floating-axis equations do not apply there, so an Euler decomposition is used instead.

    Always report your convention

    Even now, a joint angle without a stated coordinate system and rotation order is not reproducible. Naming the standard you followed, and its version, costs one sentence in a methods section and saves the reader from guessing.

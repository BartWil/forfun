Length–tension comes down to overlap

Active force comes from myosin heads pulling on actin. Too short and the filaments jam into each other. Too long and they barely overlap. Either way you get fewer working cross-bridges and less force. Peak force sits at optimal length (L₀). Past that, stretched connective tissue adds passive tension (the rising tail). Neumann; Nordin & Frankel.

Force–velocity: slow is strong, eccentric stronger

Shorten fast and force collapses along the Hill hyperbola, which is why you cannot lift a heavy load quickly. But lengthening under load (eccentric) produces more force than holding still, for less effort. Notice that power (force × velocity) peaks at an intermediate speed. That is the basis of optimal-load training. Enoka; Hill 1938.

Why a clinician cares

Manual muscle testing and strengthening depend on joint angle, because angle sets muscle length, and as the Levers page shows, angle also sets the moment arm. A muscle tested in its shortened range can test weak even when it is perfectly healthy. Eccentric loading programmes (Alfredson's for Achilles tendinopathy, the Nordic curl for hamstrings) are genuinely effective, but be careful about why. The reason is more than "eccentric force is highest". The better-supported explanations are tendon remodelling under sustained mechanical load and, for the hamstrings, an increase in fascicle length. That shifts the whole length–tension curve, so peak force is produced at a longer muscle length, exactly where the injury happens. Position is the whole curve.

A normalised Hill-type model for teaching (force in multiples of maximum isometric force F₀, velocity in multiples of V_max). Curve shapes follow the classic length–tension and force–velocity relationships, and exact numbers are illustrative. One caveat about the eccentric side. This curve plateaus near 1.6 F₀, which is what isolated muscle does. A person contracting voluntarily usually cannot express that, producing only a little above their isometric maximum, because the nervous system limits it. The gap between what muscle can do and what you can volitionally ask it to do is itself a real finding. The Ruby engine downloads ~16 MB once.

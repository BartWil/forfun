    Measure outside, solve inside

    Inverse Dynamics

    You cannot put a force sensor inside a living knee. Yet almost every paper you will read reports knee moments in newton-metres. Those numbers were never measured. They were calculated backwards, from the floor up, using nothing but the force under the foot, the motion of the limb, and the mass of its parts. That calculation is called inverse dynamics, and by the end of this page you will have done one yourself. Hover any underlined phrase to see what the source actually says.

    The problem

    The interesting forces are the ones you cannot reach

    A force plate tells you what the ground does to the body. A camera system tells you where every segment is. Neither tells you what the quadriceps is pulling with, or how hard the tibia is being pressed into the femur. As the OpenSim paper puts it, the forces generated by muscles are not generally measurable in experiments. Electromyography does not rescue you either: EMG shows when a muscle is active, not what motion it caused.

    So biomechanics does the next best thing. It takes the measurements it can make on the outside and works inward, one segment at a time, using nothing more exotic than Newton's laws. The result is not a muscle force. It is the net joint moment: the single turning effect that all the muscles, ligaments and contact forces at that joint add up to. Knowing exactly what that number does and does not contain is most of what this page is for.

    Step one

    Cut one segment free and draw everything touching it

    Take the foot. Imagine slicing through the ankle and lifting the foot away from the body. Four things act on it, and only four: the ground pushes up through the centre of pressure, gravity pulls down at the centre of mass, and across the cut the shank applies both a force and a moment. Write Newton's second law twice, once for straight-line motion and once for rotation, and the two unknowns at the ankle drop out. Hover a term below to light it up in the diagram.

    Read the small print

    The version on the slide, and where it stops working

    You will often meet inverse dynamics written compactly, as a moment of inertia times an angular acceleration minus the moment of the external force. Read carefully, that form is better than it first looks. If you take moments about the joint itself, the unknown joint reaction force drops out on its own, because a force has no moment about its own point of application. What the short form still leaves out is the weight and the inertia of every segment between that joint and the ground. At the ankle only the foot sits below, so the error is a few percent. Each step up the chain adds another neglected segment. Press Swing in the panel further down, where the foot leaves the ground: with no external force left, the short form has nothing to work with and is wrong by roughly 100%, because in swing those neglected weights and inertias are the entire answer.

    The whole chain

    Solve the foot, then stand on the answer

    Here is the part that makes it work. Once the foot is solved you know the force and moment at the ankle. By Newton's third law those act back on the shank with the sign flipped, so the shank now has only two unknowns as well, at the knee. Solve it, flip the signs again, and the thigh gives you the hip. Each answer becomes the next segment's known.

    Drag the joints to change the posture. Drag the arrowhead to change the ground reaction force, and the dot on the floor to move the centre of pressure. Every number updates exactly, by the arithmetic above. Nothing here is precomputed, and you can check any line of it by hand.

    Notice what happens when you drag the force vector so its line passes straight through a joint centre: that joint's moment collapses to almost nothing. The residue is the segment's own weight and inertia. This is the whole of inverse dynamics in one observation, and it is why the perpendicular distance from the joint to the line of the force matters more than the size of the force.

    What you have to know first

    Three ingredients, and one of them is borrowed

    1

    The force under the foot

    A force plate gives you the three components of the ground reaction force and the centre of pressure, the point on the plate through which that force acts. The centre of pressure has a property worth saying out loud: it is defined as the point where the ground applies no moment. That is why the foot's distal moment term is zero, and it is the only reason the chain can be started at all.

    2

    The motion of the segments

    Motion capture gives positions. Inverse dynamics needs accelerations, which means differentiating those positions twice. Differentiation amplifies noise brutally: a barely visible wobble in a marker trace becomes a large spike in acceleration. This is why raw coordinates are always filtered first, and why the choice of filter cut-off is a real methodological decision rather than a detail.

    3

    The mass of each segment

    Nobody weighs your shank. Its mass, the position of its centre of mass and its moment of inertia are all read from a table built on other people's bodies and scaled to your height and weight. This is the borrowed ingredient, and the one students most often accept without asking where it came from. So let us ask.

    Pick a table and watch the answer move

    Two sets of body segment parameters dominate the literature, and they disagree. One was measured on eight embalmed cadavers of elderly men. The other was measured on one hundred living young men with a gamma-ray scanner in Moscow. Switch between them and watch the joint moments from your own posture above change underneath you.

    Handle with care

    Four things the answer is not

    It is not a muscle force

    A joint moment of 90 N·m does not mean the plantarflexors produced 90 N·m. It means everything crossing the ankle summed to that. If tibialis anterior is co-contracting, the calf is producing more than 90 and the antagonist is cancelling part of it. Inverse dynamics is blind to co-contraction, and can never split one net number into individual muscles without extra assumptions.

    The errors grow as you climb

    The chain hands each segment's answer up to the next, and hands its errors up too. An error in the foot's mass affects the ankle a little, the knee more and the hip most, because every later step inherits everything before it. This is why ankle moments are the most trustworthy output of a gait lab and hip moments the least. Use the sliders above to see it: a wrong segment mass barely moves the ankle and clearly moves the hip.

    The measurements disagree with each other

    Force plate and camera are separate instruments, so the force they record and the motion they record never quite describe the same body. The OpenSim paper is blunt about it: measured forces are often dynamically inconsistent with the model kinematics. The mismatch is absorbed into a fictitious residual force and moment applied to the trunk, and in the absence of error that residual should be zero, but in practice it never is. A large residual is the honest warning light of a gait analysis.

    The skin moves and the bone does not

    Markers are stuck to skin, and skin slides over bone by up to a couple of centimetres during fast movement. That soft tissue artefact corrupts the joint centre positions, which corrupts the moment arms, which corrupts every moment you compute. It is the largest single source of error in most gait laboratories, and no amount of filtering removes it, because it is not noise. It is a real motion of the wrong thing.

    Do it for real

    The software students can actually get

    Everything on this page is a two-dimensional teaching version. Real analysis happens in three dimensions with a full musculoskeletal model, and the standard tool for that is free.

    A word on why OpenSim exists at all. Before it, most laboratories wrote their own simulation code and kept it, which meant results could not be checked by anyone else. Delp and colleagues called that out plainly: the inability to reproduce results is a major limitation to advancing the science. Their answer was to give the software away. If you take one professional habit from this page, take that one, and add the other: every simulation contains assumptions, so it is critically important to test it and establish its limitations.

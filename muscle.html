Force × distance

Muscle Levers

Hold a 5 kg dumbbell and your biceps pulls with more like 50 kg of force. Muscles attach right next to the joints they move, so they work on a tiny lever arm and pay for it in force. Drag the sliders and watch the trade-off: a joint is a lever, and torque is force × distance.

Why the huge force?

For the arm to hold still, the turning effect (torque) the muscle makes must exactly balance the torque from the weight. Torque = force × moment arm, the perpendicular distance from the joint axis to the line of pull. The weight sits ~34 cm out; the biceps pulls on a moment arm of only ~4 cm, so to match the torque its force must be about 34 ÷ 4 ≈ 8–10× the weight. Short lever arm, big force. That's the deal almost every muscle in your body makes. Measured biceps moment arms peak near 4.7 cm (Murray, Buchanan & Delp 2000).

So why build bodies this way?

Losing force buys speed and range: a muscle shortening a couple of centimetres near the joint whips the hand through a big, fast arc. The same short lever that costs force lets you throw, kick, and sprint. It also keeps limbs slim and light near the body. The price is joint load. Watch the third number climb: most of that muscle force ends up compressing the joint.

Agonist & antagonist

Curling up, the biceps is the agonist (the mover) while the triceps on the back of the arm is the antagonist, relaxing and paying out to let the motion happen. Straighten the arm against a load and the roles swap. Muscles only pull, so they come in opposing pairs, one for each direction.

It is never one muscle

This model says "biceps" because it is easier to draw, but elbow flexion is shared by at least three muscles, and they are not interchangeable. Measured on cadaver specimens (Murray, Buchanan & Delp 2000): brachioradialis has the largest flexion moment arm of all at 7.7 cm, well over the biceps' 4.7 cm, yet it has the smallest cross-sectional area. It is a long lever on a small motor. Brachialis is the opposite: a moment arm of only 2.6 cm but the largest cross-section of the flexors, a short lever on a big motor. The biceps sits between them, which is why it ends up with the greatest overall moment-generating capacity of the three. Real joints are always a committee.

And the moment arm keeps moving

Slide the moment arm and you are doing by hand what your elbow does automatically: moment arms change with joint angle, and the biceps' varies by more than 30% across the range. So joint torque = muscle force × moment arm, and both terms move as you rotate the joint. The muscle force term has its own rule, the length–tension curve, which you can play with on the Muscle Dyno. Put the two together and you have the reason a muscle tests strong at one joint angle and weak at another. For anyone doing manual muscle testing that is the most practical fact on this page.

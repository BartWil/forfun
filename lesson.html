Anatomy of a Step

One ordinary step hides a beautifully timed sequence. Scroll down and we'll walk through it together — one phase at a time — while the figure moves and the force trace keeps pace. Poke the controls whenever you like; the point is to play.

It's a loop, not a line

Biomechanists measure walking in one gait cycle: from one foot touching down to that same foot touching down again. We stretch it to 0–100% so everyone lines up, whatever their height or speed. Grab this and scrub through the whole thing:

Roughly the first ~60% is stance (foot down); the rest is swing (foot in the air).

1 · Heel strike & loading

The heel lands and the body drops onto it. Watch the force trace spike to its first peak — more than your body weight — as you catch your falling mass. The knee immediately bends a little to soften the blow, like a car's suspension. Strictly there are two phases here: the touchdown instant itself is initial contact (the first ~2% of the cycle), and the shock-absorbing bit that follows is loading response.

2 · Mid-stance — the vault

Now you roll up and over a nearly straight leg, like an inverted pendulum. The leg is at its longest, your hips at their highest — so the ground force actually dips below body weight for a moment. The knee has straightened back out.

3 · Push-off

The calf fires and the ankle drives you forward and up onto the toes. That's the force trace's second peak. This push is the engine of walking — most of the propulsion happens right here, at the end of stance.

Three rockers, one smooth roll

Physiotherapy has a name for what you just watched. Perry describes stance as three rockers — three pivots that hand the body forward instead of letting it stall over a fixed foot. Heel rocker (loading response): you land on the heel and the foot pivots about it while tibialis anterior lowers the forefoot under control — this is the rocker that fails in foot drop. Ankle rocker (mid stance): the foot is flat and the shin rotates forward over the ankle — the rocker a stiff ankle destroys. Forefoot rocker (terminal stance): the heel lifts and the body rolls over the metatarsal heads while the calf controls, then drives. Lose any one and the body must find a detour — which is exactly what you can switch on in the Gait Lab.

4 · Toe-off

The toes leave the ground and the ground force drops to zero — this leg is now flying. For the next third of the cycle it carries no load at all; it just has to swing through and get ready to land.

5 · Swing — clear the floor

Here's the knee's big bend — up to ~60°. With no ground to push on, the only job is to fold the leg up so the foot doesn't catch. A shorter, folded leg is also a faster pendulum, so it whips forward efficiently.

6 · Reaching for the next step

The knee straightens and the leg reaches forward, decelerating so the heel can land gently. The instant it touches down, the loop starts over — and the other leg is doing all of this exactly half a cycle behind.

Now you drive
